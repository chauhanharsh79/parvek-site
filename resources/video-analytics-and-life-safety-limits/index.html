Video analytics and life-safety systems: roles and limits

November 4, 2026 7 min read

This article exists to draw a line clearly, because the security industry has a habit of blurring it.

Certified life-safety systems are authoritative. Video analytics are supplementary. That ordering does not change, and no feature list reverses it.

What “certified” is actually carrying

A fire panel, a heat or smoke detector, a gas detector, a machine-safety interlock — these are engineered, type-approved and periodically inspected against published standards. The approval is a statement about failure behaviour: how the device fails, how that failure is detected, and what happens when it does.

A camera and a model have none of that. A camera can be obscured, knocked, blinded by glare or simply pointed somewhere else, and nothing in the system is obliged to notice. A detection model has a false-negative rate that varies with lighting, distance and scene — and it is not certified to any life-safety standard.

That difference is the whole argument. It is not about which detects faster in a demo.

Where video genuinely helps

Plenty, provided it is positioned honestly.

Context for responders. When a panel activates, the people responding want to know what they are walking into. A camera view of the area is immediately useful, and it changes nothing about the panel’s authority.

Earlier awareness in spaces heat takes time to reach. High bays, open yards, material stores. Visible flame or smoke in a camera view can appear before enough heat reaches a ceiling-mounted detector. This is a supplementary signal that prompts a human to look — not a substitute for the detector.

Faster location. A large site with a zoned panel tells you roughly where. A camera tells you exactly where.

The record afterwards. What happened, in what order, and how the response unfolded. Valuable for investigation and for insurance.

Person-down awareness in low-traffic areas. A person detected on the floor in a space worked alone is worth raising. It is a security event that brings a human to look. It is not a medical device, it does not assess the person, and it does not replace a clinical or emergency procedure.

Where it must not be positioned

As a replacement for a certified detector. Not in a quote, not on a slide, not implied by omission.

As a reason to reduce certified coverage. If anyone is using video analytics to justify fewer detectors, stop and involve your fire engineer.

As a medical or emergency-response capability. Person-down detection raises an alert. Everything after that is your procedure.

As an override of a safety interlock. Machine-safety systems are interlocked for a reason and sit outside this entirely.

Questions worth asking any vendor

  • Is this device or feature certified to a life-safety standard? Which one?
  • What happens when the camera is obscured, misaligned or offline — is that detected and raised?
  • What is the false-negative behaviour in low light, in smoke, at the distances on my site?
  • Would you put this claim in the contract?

That last one is the most efficient question in the entire evaluation. Marketing language that will not survive being written into a contract tells you what it is.

How we position it

PARVEK detects visible flame and smoke in a camera view, and it is offered as a supplementary layer. Wherever it appears on this site it carries the same note: supplementary to certified fire and life-safety detection, never a replacement for it. Visible flame or smoke has to be in the camera view, and statutory detection and suppression systems remain authoritative.

The same applies to person-down detection, PPE monitoring and anything else touching worker safety. These support visual review and faster human response. They are not a replacement for safety procedures or certified devices, and the assessment will say so again in writing before anything is deployed.

Why we are this blunt about it

Partly because it is correct. Partly because a vendor willing to overstate a life-safety capability in a sales conversation will overstate the others too — and if you are evaluating physical security systems, the way a supplier talks about the claims they cannot make is a reasonable proxy for how much to trust the ones they do.
